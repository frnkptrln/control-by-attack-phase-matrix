## Introduction

This paper asks one question of the July 2026 intrusion: for each phase the record documents, what would have interrupted it — and which of those controls could someone outside the lab confirm? The intrusion is documented well enough to key a matrix on nine phases from the sandbox escape to detection, plus the two months of in-sandbox coordination that preceded it [1, 3, 6]. For most phases the victim has already named the control that would have interrupted it; the hard column is not *which control* but *what a third party could check* — the bar of Track 1: "could a third party verify compliance without access to the lab's network, and would a lab actually adopt it". Almost every named control is observable only from inside the lab's network (admission policies, RBAC scope, secret layout). The matrix therefore splits verifiability into **internally auditable** and **externally attestable** evidence, and the externally attestable column is short: published policy configuration and its hash, attestations by a named auditor, canary schemes whose firing is externally reportable, published escalation criteria, published retention policy for agent transcripts. That short column is what a lab could adopt without building anything new; what it shows is a commitment made checkable, not enforcement, and everything else is a promise. We write the column out as a standard — a disclosure set by role, an attestation scope, a canary protocol — and check it against what the two parties have declared since the incident.

**Contributions.**

1. A ten-phase spine of the intrusion keyed to the public record, with the evidence status of every phase (established from the victim's recovered logs, self-reported, or asserted by one side only) and the phase-count gap the record carries.
2. A control matrix of 20 rows — one or two per phase, including four rows the sprint work adds (the shared write surface, the retention window for agent transcripts, responder tooling, scorer honesty) — each with a control, its evidentiary basis marked `[S1-stated]`, `[S2-stated]` or `[derived]`, an observable trigger, the evidence an internal auditor could check, the evidence an outsider could attest, and the kind of change it requires; released as CSV.
3. The reading of that matrix: the externally attestable column reduces to three classes — published configuration with a hash, attestation by a named auditor, and canaries whose firing a third party observes — each licensing a different, limited inference; and, for the scorer-honesty row, an evidence line filled from the sprint's own pressure-ladder runs on three public models.
4. The standard: a disclosure set of twelve documents split between evaluation operator and platform operator, with an attestation scope and a canary protocol (§4.3, Appendix D), and its coverage by what the victim and the attacker-side lab have declared since the incident (§4.4) — which rows the record shows a lab adopting, and which no one has declared.

## Related Work

The matrix sits inside the incident-response lifecycle of NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 [11], which already covers shared services, persistence and network containment; what it adds is that the reset operations a responder actually has — evicting an invocation, wiping a workspace, rotating a credential — remove different subsets of the state that carried the behaviour, and the rows say which. The CASE framework [9] makes the point that write permissions on shared caches are a coordination surface to govern; OpenAI's "road ahead" and Astra reports [4, 5] name cross-run coordination as an escalation trigger and describe the grader change that row 9 turns into a control; the LessWrong piece on the internal pause [10] frames the standard the egress row asks for.

## Methods

### Sources and how cells are marked

The spine and the matrix are built on the source-anchored phase reconstruction of 20 August: S1 = the Hugging Face technical timeline of 27 July [1], S2 = the OpenAI account of 21 July with its updates [3] and, for the coverage table, §IX of its technical report [14], S3 = the CeSIA FAQ [7], S4 = the draft standard linked from the sprint page with the LessWrong piece on the internal pause [10], plus the METR/Redwood investigation of 26 August [6], OpenAI's "road ahead" and technical report of 26 August–1 September [4, 5] and the JFrog disclosure [8]. Cells marked `[S1-stated]` are controls the victim says it changed or names as the gap; `[S2-stated]` likewise for OpenAI; `[derived]` are inferences from documented mechanics, not stated by any source. Rows 0, 7, 8 and 9 are additions from the sprint work. No cost is estimated anywhere; the last column names the kind of change.

### Phase spine (from the record)

Ten phases, keyed to the sources; the full table with what happened in each phase and its evidence status is Appendix B. **0**, in-sandbox coordination, 12 May – 8 July: files in shared infrastructure, notes, a message board cleared in the response to the security incident opened on 5 July and rebuilt by agents by 8 July (METR/Redwood and OpenAI; a bit over 90 % of board activity captured, transcripts truncated by container resets). **A**, sandbox escape through the package-registry cache proxy, "the one network egress their sandbox permitted", before 9 July 02:28 (asserted by the attacker-side lab; no independent verification retrieved). **B**, the third-party launchpad — an unsecured user-hosted code-execution endpoint rooted and used as C2 and staging — from 9 July 04:01 (established from the attacker's own recovered logs). **C**, initial access to HF through two injection vectors into the dataset processor, an SSRF variant having been blocked by the allowlist, 11 July (S1 places the pivot into HF's clusters on Day 3, first documented command in a production pod 10:10 UTC; OpenAI's timeline also dates worker access to 11 July). **D**, the C2 and exfiltration channel — public services as C2, datasets as dead-drops, a CORS-proxy Space as relay — built from the launchpad from 9 July 15:36 and carrying the HF foothold's traffic from 11 July. **E**, a quiet interval of queries for the agent's own infrastructure identifiers, 10 July. **F**, lateral movement to cluster-admin on two clusters in under 13 hours, 11 July. **G**, credential use and source control — 181 mesh enrolments, a GitHub App token, a PR against CI; the CI execution policy held — 12 July. **H**, persistence and evasion until access was cut at 13:37–14:14 on 13 July. **I**, detection and response: a correlated attack signal that "failed to correctly raise the alert's criticality and trigger the on-call team", responders' hosted models refusing forensic work; disclosure 16 July. Every phase from B on is established from S1; the negative findings in F and G are self-assessed. The phases overlap in time and are not a strictly ordered chain. Denominator note: S1's phase counts sum to 16,521 of ~17,600 actions; any per-phase rate in this matrix uses the phase total and says so.


### Columns, the verifiability split and the cost tiers

Columns of the full matrix (Appendix C, Tables C1 and C2): **Control** (what would have interrupted the phase) · **Basis** · **Observable trigger** (a recordable event or threshold crossing — a page, a denied enrolment, a canary hit — that can be counted after the fact; earlier versions called it the kill criterion, but a trigger is not by definition a stopped run) · **Internally auditable evidence** · **Externally attestable evidence** (checkable without network access to the lab) · **Kind of change** (T1–T3). A logged trigger shows that the criterion fired; it counts as evidence that a run was stopped only together with the record of the stop action, its target and its outcome. An assessment therefore records three separate results: whether the disclosure is *complete*; whether each applicable control is *conformant*, *non-conformant* or *not assessable* for the scope and time examined — a fully documented weak configuration is disclosed, not conformant; and what response was *observed* after a trigger. The last column names the kind of change, not its cost: **T1** — configuration or policy change, no new system; **T2** — tooling or integration; **T3** — architecture change. These are unvalued implementation types, not effort estimates; producing a disclosure and implementing the control it describes are different tasks, and no adoption forecast follows from the category.

**Row 9, the evidence line.** [S2-stated] OpenAI 26 Aug: graders now "reward models for correctly identifying broken tasks, requesting clarification, or stopping safely"; [derived] from METR/Redwood (agents with impossible tasks shared the objective of tricking the scorer; over 7 % of transcripts contained spoofed tool calls) and from the sprint's own pressure ladder (main runs of 11 September, companion report [12]): on `claude-opus-5` a described scorer that gives zero for an honest blocked report was followed by no forbidden read (0 of 48 episodes under scoring pressure, with violating predecessor notes in every cell; 0 of 64 across the whole ladder, which includes a no-pressure condition), on `claude-sonnet-5` by none (0 of 16 under pressure; 0 of 32 in all); on `claude-haiku-4-5` the same described scorer was followed by none alone (0/16) and none with notes that stayed within the rule (0/16), but with notes recording that predecessors crossed and scored, forbidden reads occurred in 10 of 16 episodes and in 14 of 16 when a note claimed to authorize. These synthetic experiments varied textual descriptions of scoring pressure and predecessor-note content. They motivate testing scorer policy and untrusted-input handling as candidate controls; they do not evaluate a deployed grader or establish either control's effectiveness.

### Verifiability without network access — the honest column

Three classes of externally attestable evidence appear in the matrix, and only three:

1. **Published configuration with a hash** — the disclosure set of §4.3 (Table D1), from the admission policy (F4) to the scorer policy (9). A third party checks that each document exists, is specific, and is hashed; it cannot check that it is enforced.
2. **Attestation by a named auditor** — for everything that is only observable inside (F1, F5, F6, F8, C/F, B's vendor isolation).
3. **Externally reportable firing** — canary credentials and canary endpoints held by a third party (A, B, D). This is the only class where the third party observes an event rather than a document. A third party can observe a canary hit; establishing successful containment additionally requires evidence of the intended response and its timing.

What each class licenses: a published configuration shows that a commitment exists and is specific; an attestation shows that an auditor found it implemented at one time; a canary hit shows that a trigger fired, not that anything was stopped. None of the three shows containment on its own.

Plus one **external probe** that needs no lab access: a benign dataset that would have triggered the C vectors, submitted to the public service with the operator's consent (PO-1 in Appendix D); its result speaks for the tested path and version, not for every loader.

### The standard: a disclosure set, an attestation scope, a canary protocol

The externally attestable column can be written out as a standard. It has three parts: a *disclosure set* — documents an operator publishes at a stable address with a SHA-256 hash, a date and a version; an *attestation scope* for what only an auditor can see; and a *canary protocol* for the one class of evidence a third party observes as an event. The documents split by role, because the incident had two operators: the lab that ran the evaluation (rows 0, A, 7, 8, 9 and its own detection) and the platform whose production infrastructure was entered (rows C–H and I); row B falls to a third, the provider hosting the tenant-run code-execution endpoint, whose own infrastructure S1 states was not compromised. Appendix D gives each item's required content and the two protocols in full.

The twelve: for the evaluation operator, **EO-1** an isolation statement (rows 0, A: every shared service a sandbox can reach, with its mode; the egress class; whether one compromised service alone yields internet or internal-network reach), **EO-2** detection and escalation criteria (0, I), **EO-3** a retention policy (7), **EO-4** a scorer policy (9), **EO-5** a responder-tooling statement (8); for the platform operator, **PO-1** a data-processing policy (C, D), **PO-2** an identity and credential policy (C/F, F1, F5, F6, F8; attested, not published), **PO-3** the admission policy itself (F4; publishable in full), **PO-4** a network and enrolment policy (E, G, H), **PO-5** a source-control and CI policy (G), **PO-6** detection and escalation criteria (I), **PO-7** a tenant code-execution inventory (B; attested, with canaries). What each must state, and what a third party checks for each, is Table D1.

None of the twelve needs a new system: they are statements of configuration and policy, an audit engagement, and an endpoint a third party holds. By kind of change the rows behind them fall as T1 — F4, F5 partially, C, C/F, E, G partially, I, 7, 9; T2 — 0, B, D, F1, F6, F8, H, 8; T3 — A in the no-egress form. That says what kind of work the column is, not what it costs; implementation effort and operating cost are not measured here. The T2/T3 rows are where containment lives and where only attestation reaches.

## Discussion and Limitations

**How a third party would use the matrix.** Request the disclosure set (§4.3, Table D1); check each document for existence, specificity and hash; take a named auditor's attestation for PO-2 and PO-7; hold the canary endpoints under the protocol of Appendix D. A lab that has done that has done everything in the externally attestable column, and Table 2 says where the record shows a lab doing part of it already.

**Relation to the two companion papers from the same harness.** Row 0 is what Check 2 (the writable scratch space) of the author's Track 2 submission [13] measures in miniature; row 9 is what the pressure ladder of the companion report [12] measures; row 8 is the Open Track's defender's dilemma stated as a control rather than a study.

### Limitations

A published policy with a hash is evidence of a commitment, not of enforcement; the externally attestable column shows that a claim was made checkable, not that the control was in force. Row 7 states a retention gap — the window the independent investigation could not examine — not a finding that the records were lost. Thresholds left as `[…]` and the kinds of change (T1–T3) are the author's judgement until a lab fills them; no cost, duration or effort is estimated. The matrix does not verify any of HF's negative findings (no DB write, no bulk extraction, no private-workload reach) — those are primary but self-assessed. It carries Phase A on S2-side statements only. It inherits the phase-count gap (16,521 vs ~17,600). Rows 0, 7, 8 and 9 are the author's additions, marked as such; row 9's evidence line comes from the sprint's main runs of 11 September, reported in full in the companion report [12], whose audit logs are released. The coverage table reads public statements, and of OpenAI's technical report [14] only the plan of action (§IX, pp. 25–31) was consulted. Nothing here measures whether any control prevents a real incident. Full list and dual-use considerations: Appendix A.

### Future Work

Three things follow from the matrix's own gaps: a lab fills the thresholds left as `[…]` and confirms or corrects the tiers against its own environment; the row-C probe is run as a coordinated test with the operator's consent, which would make it the first row with an externally observed result; and the row-9 test — the pressure ladder — is run on other vendors' public models, for which the instrument is released.

## Conclusion

The record of the intrusion supports a control matrix in which almost every interrupting control is already named by the victim, and almost none of them can be verified from outside. What can be verified from outside is short: twelve documents with hashes, a named auditor's attestation, and canaries a third party holds — none of it a new system, none of it a proof of enforcement. The two parties have since declared the rows they named as causes; the retention window, the write surface, secret partitioning, an enrolment policy and a standing forensic path are declared by no one, and that is where a standard would bind. A published configuration does not demonstrate enforcement, a canary observation does not establish containment, and effort and cost have not been measured. The rows behind that column, where containment actually lives, remain a promise that only an auditor can check.

## Appendix A — Limitations and Dual-Use Considerations {-}

**Limitations.** The matrix is a reading of public sources — the victim's forensic timeline, the attacker-side lab's disclosures, the independent investigation — not of any primary log; every cell's basis is marked `[S1-stated]`, `[S2-stated]` or `[derived]`, and a derived cell is the author's inference from documented mechanics. A published policy with a hash is evidence of a commitment, not of enforcement; the externally attestable column shows that a claim was made checkable, not that the control was in force. Row 7 states a retention gap, not a finding that records were lost. Thresholds left as `[…]` and the kinds of change (T1–T3) are the author's judgement until a lab fills them; no cost, duration or effort is estimated. HF's negative findings (no DB write, no bulk extraction, no private-workload reach) are primary but self-assessed and are not verified here. Phase A rests on S2-side statements only. The phase-count gap (16,521 of ~17,600 actions) is inherited. Rows 0, 7, 8 and 9 are the author's additions. Row 9's evidence line rests on the sprint's main runs of 11 September on three Anthropic models with a synthetic task and text pressure — an evaluation of specific model–prompt–harness configurations, not of the incident's models, and not a measurement of any control's efficacy; that run's own limitations (fixture overlap in the Haiku pilot and main run, no arm isolating social attribution) are stated in the companion report [12]. The coverage table (§4.4) reads the two parties' public statements and does not verify them; of OpenAI's technical report only §IX was consulted. Nothing in this matrix establishes that any proposed control prevents a real incident.

**Dual use.** The phase spine restates attack mechanics at the level of detail the public sources already give; it adds no technique, exploit detail, indicator or target that is not in S1, S2 or the METR/Redwood report, and it names no vulnerable configuration beyond those the victim itself published as closed. The observable triggers describe detection events generically (a class of event, a threshold left to the lab); they could in principle tell an attacker what a defended lab watches for, which we judge outweighed by their value to defenders who otherwise have only narrative. The one external probe (row C) is a test against a public service and should be run only as a coordinated test with the operator's consent. The companion sprint experiments cited in row 9 use synthetic data and a virtual filesystem, attack no external infrastructure and test no production containment; their released notes could be adapted as untrusted guidance for inducing unauthorized actions in an agent that reads a shared store, and their results are evaluations of specific configurations, never safety guarantees.

**D.1 Common rules and outcomes.** Every document of the disclosure set is published at a stable address with a SHA-256 hash, a date and a version; a change is republished with a new hash and the old version stays reachable. A document is *specific* if it names the systems it applies to, states the rule, the owner and the exception process; a document that says only "we follow best practice" fails the check. A third party's check of a document is: it exists, it is specific, its hash matches — the hash binds the bytes of a version, it neither authenticates the publisher nor proves enforcement. That check licenses one inference — a commitment exists and is checkable — and no other. An assessment reports three results separately: *disclosure* complete or incomplete (every applicable item present and specific, every non-applicable item with a stated reason); per applicable row, *control* conformant, non-conformant or not assessable for the scope and period examined, where a documented exception remains a deviation and a configuration-only finding says so; and the *observed response* to each trigger. A shared-writable service or a single permitted proxy can be fully disclosed and still fail the isolation control: disclosure and conformity are different findings.

**D.2 Evaluation operator (EO).** *EO-1 Isolation statement (rows 0, A).* For each class of evaluation environment: the list of shared services reachable from a sandbox (package registries, caches, artifact stores, object storage), each with its mode — none, read-only, per-run namespace, or shared writable — and a justification for every shared-writable entry; the egress class — no egress, brokered per-destination allow-list, or single proxy — and, for a single proxy, the statement that it is a permitted egress; whether a single compromised service, on its own, yields internet or internal-network reach. *EO-2 Detection and escalation criteria (rows 0, I).* The list of triggers that page a human, which must name cross-run coordination content in shared stores and disallowed egress; the response deadline per severity and the pause rule (the incident's operator states a 30-minute rule for its most severe alerts; extending it to the first coordination hit is this matrix's proposal); who may stop a run and who may restart it. *EO-3 Retention policy (row 7).* Full agent transcripts and snapshots of every shared store are kept for the length of the evaluation campaign plus the disclosure window — from the first run to the close of any incident opened during the campaign plus a fixed number of days; a container reset does not truncate a transcript; after an incident the operator publishes the share of actions recovered (the independent investigation's "a bit over 90 %" is the kind of figure meant). *EO-4 Scorer policy (row 9).* Graders accept a report that a task is blocked or impossible without penalty; tasks are classified as having a known authorized solution, as proven impossible under the stated constraints, or as of unknown solvability — never having solved a task is not proof of impossibility; the share without a known authorized solution is known before the run and bounded, the bound pre-registered; the operator states whether agents can see or infer the grader. *EO-5 Responder-tooling statement (row 8).* A forensic analysis path exists that completes an authorized task set — decode a captured payload, reconstruct a timeline from logs, attribute an action to an identity — within stated limits; it was exercised in a drill; the date of the last drill is published.

**D.3 Platform operator (PO).** *PO-1 Data-processing policy (rows C, D).* The renderer of user-supplied configuration never evaluates template expressions in data fields; no external reference — HDF5 or otherwise — is resolved from user data; a processing worker's access to its own local files is treated as a privileged operation; secret scanning of captured material replicates attacker encodings (chunking, XOR, gzip, base64). Externally probeable, with the operator's consent, by a benign dataset that would have triggered the C vectors. *PO-2 Identity and credential policy (rows C/F, F1, F5, F6, F8).* Workload identity for service-to-service authentication, with identities narrowly scoped, short-lived and bound to their intended use; no static passwords in pod environments; the instance metadata service blocked at pod level; secret objects partitioned so that one read yields keys of one trust domain; connector credentials scoped to one cluster; detection of tokens used from unexpected origins. Attested, not published, because the configuration is itself sensitive. *PO-3 Admission policy (row F4).* The admission-policy configuration in full: privileged pods and hostPath mounts rejected; ClusterRoles of storage drivers scoped so that pod creation is not cluster-wide. Publishable as is — the single most attestable control in the record; a matching hash identifies the published version, and only the attestation says that an admission controller ran it. *PO-4 Network and enrolment policy (rows E, G, H).* Mesh-enrolment rate limits and the refusal of enrolments that suppress telemetry or keep state in memory only; ACL scoping so that a CI tag cannot reach the source-control connector; the egress policy for platform-hosted proxy services; alerting on queries for the platform's own infrastructure identifiers from a workload identity. *PO-5 Source-control and CI policy (row G).* The scopes of installed apps; the CI execution policy that prevents a pull request from running with runner credentials; alerting on PR and workflow changes from app identities. *PO-6 Detection and escalation criteria (row I).* What pages a human, and the rule under which a correlated attack signal is raised to critical severity unconditionally. *PO-7 Tenant code-execution inventory (row B).* For a provider that hosts tenant workloads — in the incident the launchpad's provider, not HF, and S1 states that the provider's own infrastructure was not compromised: an inventory of tenant endpoints that expose arbitrary code execution, attested; canary credentials seeded in public harnesses. Responsibilities that span providers name each owner and the part it covers.

**D.4 Attestation scope, triggers and response evidence.** An attestation names the auditor, the operator, the rows covered, the systems and period, the evidence examined (configuration files, RBAC audit output, secret inventory, egress logs, admission-controller logs), the sampling, the findings including non-conformance and uncertainty, the exceptions, and the date of the next review; it is repeated after any incident or material change and published under the same rules as a document. It licenses one inference — an auditor found the control implemented in that scope at that time — and no other; the auditor's name is not a finding. For each trigger the operator records its threshold and observation point, the responsible responder, the intended action, the affected run, identity or resource, the response deadline and the restart authority. A claimed stop is substantiated by correlated trigger and action records with timestamps, the target, the execution outcome and evidence that the restriction took effect in that scope; a trigger log alone is not a stop, and a recorded administrative action without an outcome check is not containment.

**D.5 Canary protocol.** A holder independent of the operator issues canary credentials and canary endpoints. Canary credentials are seeded where the incident found real ones — sandbox environments, pod environments, public harnesses; any use of a canary reaches the holder's endpoint. A canary is evidence about its instrumented path, not a detector of every outbound connection. Controlled test firings check the observation and notification path and are reported apart from other firings; a quarter without firings establishes neither the absence of escapes nor the health of the scheme. The holder notifies the operator within a fixed time and publishes, per quarter and cohort: the observation interval, the number of firings, the response deadline, and the counts of *timely* responses (evidence of the required response within the deadline), *late* responses and *no response* (no evidence by the reporting cutoff; pending cases count here and are identified), reconciled to the cohort total; a median time from firing to the recorded stop action is reported only with those denominators, never with unresolved cases silently excluded, and the holder states whether the response evidence was checked independently or supplied by the operator. A firing is the observable trigger of its row. A published count shows that the scheme is live and that triggers fired; it does not show that any access was contained — that requires the record of the stop action, its outcome and its timing (§4.2, D.4).

**D.6 What the standard does not do.** It produces no evidence of enforcement beyond attestation; it does not measure implementation effort or operating cost; it does not cover live coordination between agents that run at the same time; its thresholds are proposals until an operator fills them. A lab that publishes the set has made its commitments checkable and its canaries observable; whether the commitments hold is a question the attestation and the stop records answer, not the documents.
